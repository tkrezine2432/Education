This is a student-run club

The students run the Cyber Cougars. Students propose what the club does, students vote on it, and students organize and facilitate the meetings. What the club looks like this year is decided by the people who show up and take it on.

The advisor advises. That is the whole of the role — to help, to facilitate, and to answer questions. Not to run the club and not to decide for it. If you want the club to be something, the way that happens is you propose it and the members vote.

▸ Thursday What happens at the meeting 5 items

Every item on this agenda is settled by the people in the room

Nothing here is announced as already decided. Amendments pass or fail by vote, officer nominations come from the floor, the activity calendar is filled from proposals, and both money questions go to a vote. If you want the club to run a build night, a capture-the-flag, a certification study group, or a site visit, this is the meeting where that happens.

On the agenda

  1. Review the club constitution and vote on amendments We go through the current constitution and vote on any proposed changes — including settling what "good standing" actually requires, which was left unclear last year. Read it before the meeting; the link is at the top of this page.
  2. Open officer nominations, and elect if we are able We take nominations and volunteers from the floor. You may put yourself forward. Whether we actually vote is a separate question — see below.
  3. Decide activities for the coming weeks We set what actually happens on the club activity nights. Proposals from the floor are how the calendar gets filled.
  4. Vote on a proposed increase to club dues A proposal to raise dues from $5.00 to $10.00 per semester is on the table for discussion and a vote.
  5. Vote on funding refreshments for meeting nights Whether the club approves spending from its account on pizza and refreshments at activity nights.
No promises on the officer vote

Whether officers are actually elected on Thursday depends on three things we cannot know in advance: whether enough members attend to meet quorum, whether enough people are nominated to fill the roles, and whether there is time left after the rest of the agenda. Any of those can come up short, and if one does the election carries to a later meeting. Nominations still open on Thursday either way, so it is worth being there. Quorum is defined in the constitution.

Proposing an amendment

You can raise an amendment from the floor at the meeting, but sending it ahead by email gives everyone time to think about it before the vote — and a proposal people have already read tends to get a fairer hearing than one they meet for the first time as it is being voted on.

▸ Why join Membership, and your transcript Good standing

Membership is recorded on your college transcript

If you are new, this is the part worth knowing. Membership in the Cyber Cougars — and membership in good standing — is recorded in your Mid-State transcript. It is not an informal club roster. It follows you out of here, onto the record an employer or a transfer institution can ask to see.

"In good standing" is defined in the constitution, not by us at the meeting. If you want the credit, read what it actually requires — the link is at the top of this page. That is a second reason to read it before Thursday.

And right now it is not clear enough

This came up last year and never got settled: does one failed class put a member out of good standing, or is the line a GPA below 2.0? The constitution as written does not answer that cleanly, and the difference is not academic — it decides who keeps the transcript credit and who loses it.

Pinning it down is part of Thursday's constitution review. It is worth arriving with an opinion, because whichever way the members settle it, that is the rule everyone lives under for the year.

▸ Pro points Volunteering, and Tech Con 2.1 November 13

The club gets asked to supply volunteers for events, and volunteering at them can earn pro points. Pro points are part of your program and count toward graduation, so this is not purely goodwill — it is one of the few ways club involvement feeds directly back into your degree.

The next one is Tech Con 2.1 — Friday, November 13, 2026, 8:00 AM to 3:30 PM. Tech Con is an outreach event Mid-State runs every year: high school students come in for a full day of hands-on activities to find out what IT actually is. The club is asked to help make it work.

Volunteers help with setup and then run stations. Past ones have included:

  • Lock picking
  • Soldering
  • Hacking
  • Computer coding challenges
  • The retro gaming area
  • Stickers
  • Social media

That is not the whole list — there is a wide range of jobs on the day, and they get assigned closer to the event. You do not need to be an expert at a station to run one. You need to be a step ahead of a high schooler who has never seen it before, and a few of these are things the club can practice on an activity night first.

It is also worth saying what this is like from the other side: for a lot of those high school students, the person running the lock-picking table is the first person they have met who does IT. That is most of why the event works.

Calls for volunteers go out at club meetings, which is a practical reason to be on the roster and paying attention even in weeks when nothing else is happening. Volunteering is always optional and always your choice. If you are unsure what counts toward pro points in your own program, check with your advisor rather than assuming — requirements are not identical across programs.

▸ How meetings run Robert’s Rules — the short version Article V §4

Article V, Section 4 names Robert’s Rules of Order as the club’s parliamentary authority. In practice, meetings are run loosely following those rules rather than to the letter — enough structure that decisions are clear and everyone gets heard, without turning a club meeting into a courtroom. Nobody will be ruled out of order for saying something the wrong way. This is here so the words mean something to you when you hear them.

Getting a decision made

  1. Someone makes a motion. “I move that the club buy a soldering kit.” A motion is a specific proposal, not a general idea.
  2. Someone else seconds it. That only means a second person thinks it is worth discussing. It is not agreement.
  3. The club discusses it. The chair recognises people in turn. Speak to the motion on the table, not to something else.
  4. The club votes. Most things pass on a simple majority of those voting. Amendments to the constitution need two-thirds (Article X §1).

Changing a motion before you vote on it

“I move to amend the motion to say $50 rather than $100.” The amendment gets seconded, discussed and voted on first. Then the club votes on the motion as amended. This is how a nearly-right proposal gets fixed instead of voted down.

Terms you will actually hear

  • Table it — set the question aside for now. Used when the club needs information it does not have yet.
  • Point of order — something is being done wrong. Anyone can raise it, without waiting to be recognised.
  • Point of information — a question about the facts, not an argument.
  • Call the question — a request to stop discussing and vote. It is a request, not a command; the club decides.
  • Abstain — decline to vote either way. Always allowed.
  • Quorum — the minimum attendance needed for votes to count. Here it is fifty percent plus one of paid members (Article V §3).

Who runs it

The President presides and votes only to break a tie (Article IV §1). The Secretary records what was decided (Article IV §3). If the President is absent, the Vice-President takes it (Article IV §2).

The one rule worth remembering

If you want something to happen, say it as a motion. An idea raised in discussion is a comment; a motion is a thing the club has to decide about. That single difference is most of what Robert’s Rules is for, and it is the part that matters in a club this size.
