This is a student-run club

The students run the Cyber Cougars. Students propose what the club does, students vote on it, and students organize and facilitate the meetings. What the club looks like this year is decided by the people who show up and take it on.

The advisor advises. That is the whole of the role — to help, to facilitate, and to answer questions. Not to run the club and not to decide for it. If you want the club to be something, the way that happens is you propose it and the members vote.

  3. Article III, Sections 3, 4 and 5 — Let anyone present nominate, and fix the election sequence Decided at this meeting. Anyone present may nominate; the election follows a nomination window.
    What this is aboutTwo-thirds majority (Article X §1) · notice not yet given

    The problem. As written, the club cannot seat officers in a fall semester. Section 3 requires a nomination to come from a paid member in good standing and to be seconded by another. Section 4 puts the election at the first meeting of the year. At that meeting, dues have not been collected — so there is nobody who can nominate, nobody who can second, and no way to elect the officers the club needs in order to function. The two clauses only work in a year the club does not have to start from scratch. Section 4 also names the same meeting twice, so the two-step election it describes does nothing.

    Proposed. Separate nominating from standing for office. Anyone attending a meeting or an activity night may nominate and second — it costs nothing and it is what unblocks the first semester. Dues and eligibility are tested on the nominee, at the election, which is where they actually matter, because officers hold the club's records and money. Nominations open across the first two gatherings of the year, of either kind, and the election is held at the first business meeting after they close. A seat nobody stands for reopens and is filled later by the same process. Officer terms begin on election.

▸ What they are Club business meetings Every other week

Every item at a business meeting is settled by the people in the room

Nothing is announced as already decided. Amendments are argued and voted by the members, officer nominations come from the floor, and the activity calendar is filled from proposals. If you want the club to run a build night, a capture-the-flag, a certification study group, or a site visit, a business meeting is where that happens — and it happens because somebody in the room said it.

A business meeting is where the club decides things. They run at midday, every other week, on Microsoft Teams, and they alternate with the evening activity nights so the two never fall on the same day. An hour is the normal length; they end when the agenda is done. Which day of the week they fall on is set each semester by what the members and the advisor can make — the current day, times and dates are on the business meeting card at the top of this page.

Anyone can come. You do not have to be a member to attend, to speak, or to propose something — and a meeting is the cheapest way to find out what the club actually is before paying dues. Voting is for members, and some decisions need a set share of the paid membership to be valid at all, which is why the roster matters more than it looks.

Getting something on the agenda is not a process. Tell the advisor or an officer before the meeting and it goes on, or raise it from the floor on the day. The club runs on what members propose; an agenda nobody added anything to is a short meeting, not a well-organised one.

Meetings are held on Teams so that members who are working, at another campus, or online can be in the room. The join link and the full list of dates are on the business meeting card at the top of this page.

Where the agenda is

The running order for the next business meeting is on the Next business meeting card at the top of this page, with the detail behind each item. This section is about how a meeting works, not what is on the next one.

Proposing an amendment

You can raise an amendment from the floor at any business meeting. Anything raised there gets the same treatment as the proposals already listed: written notice goes out to every member, and the vote happens at a later meeting, at least five days after the notice. That waiting period is Article X §2, and it is not a formality — it is what stops a rule changing before the members who live under it have heard about it.

An amendment can still change between the two meetings. That is the point of discussing it first: wording that is nearly right gets fixed rather than voted down. Sending a proposal ahead by email gives people even longer with it, and a proposal people have already read tends to get a fairer hearing.

How the club runs a meeting

Club meetings run on Robert's Rules of Order — the same procedure most committees and boards use. It is what decides how a motion is made, how it gets seconded, how it is debated, and how a vote is taken and declared.

The club follows them loosely, and says so. Run strictly, a one-hour student meeting would spend most of itself on procedure. What the club keeps is the part that matters: a motion, a second, a vote, and a result somebody wrote down.

▸ How meetings run Learn more about Robert's Rules Article V §4

Article V, Section 4 names Robert’s Rules of Order as the club’s parliamentary authority. In practice, meetings are run loosely following those rules rather than to the letter — enough structure that decisions are clear and everyone gets heard, without turning a club meeting into a courtroom. Nobody will be ruled out of order for saying something the wrong way. This is here so the words mean something to you when you hear them.

Getting a decision made

  1. Someone makes a motion. “I move that the club buy a soldering kit.” A motion is a specific proposal, not a general idea.
  2. Someone else seconds it. That only means a second person thinks it is worth discussing. It is not agreement.
  3. The club discusses it. The chair recognises people in turn. Speak to the motion on the table, not to something else.
  4. The club votes. Most things pass on a simple majority of those voting. Amendments to the constitution need two-thirds (Article X §1).

Changing a motion before you vote on it

“I move to amend the motion to say $50 rather than $100.” The amendment gets seconded, discussed and voted on first. Then the club votes on the motion as amended. This is how a nearly-right proposal gets fixed instead of voted down.

Terms you will actually hear

  • Table it — set the question aside for now. Used when the club needs information it does not have yet.
  • Point of order — something is being done wrong. Anyone can raise it, without waiting to be recognised.
  • Point of information — a question about the facts, not an argument.
  • Call the question — a request to stop discussing and vote. It is a request, not a command; the club decides.
  • Abstain — decline to vote either way. Always allowed.
  • Quorum — the minimum attendance needed for votes to count. Here it is fifty percent plus one of paid members (Article V §3).

Who runs it

The President presides and votes only to break a tie (Article IV §1). The Secretary records what was decided (Article IV §3). If the President is absent, the Vice-President takes it (Article IV §2).

The one rule worth remembering

If you want something to happen, say it as a motion. An idea raised in discussion is a comment; a motion is a thing the club has to decide about. That single difference is most of what Robert’s Rules is for, and it is the part that matters in a club this size.

▸ What they are Club activity nights Every other week

Activity nights are the hands-on, social half of what the club does. They run in the evening, every other week, alternating with the midday business meetings, so most weeks in a semester carry one or the other. Which day of the week, and the start time, are set each semester by what the members and the advisor can make.

Activity nights are held at either Stevens Point or Wisconsin Rapids, depending on where the active members are that semester and what the students' and the advisor's schedules allow. Neither campus is the permanent home. If getting to one of them is what would stop you joining, say so at a meeting — that is a members' decision, and it cannot be made properly by people who do not know you exist.

What happens on them

  • Guest speakers — people doing the work, talking about what it is actually like, including the parts that are not in a careers talk.
  • Gaming, digital and analog — consoles, PCs, board and card games. It runs on what people bring.
  • Builds and repairs — putting machines together, fixing them, and taking apart the ones that are past fixing.
  • Off-campus outings — when the club votes to fund one.

Nothing on that list happens because it is on that list. The calendar is filled from proposals made at business meetings, so an activity night is whatever somebody proposed and the members agreed to. If you want the club to run something, proposing it at a business meeting is the whole procedure.

The dates for the current term, including the weeks the club cannot meet, are on the activity night card at the top of this page.

Membership is recorded on your college transcript

If you are new, this is the part worth knowing. Membership in the Cyber Cougars — and membership in good standing — is recorded in your Mid-State transcript. It is not an informal club roster. It follows you out of here, onto the record an employer or a transfer institution can ask to see.

What "in good standing" requires is set by the constitution, not by whoever is chairing — which is why it matters that the constitution does not yet say. ▸ Pro points Volunteering, and what it earns you Counts toward your program

The club gets asked to supply volunteers for college events, and volunteering at them can earn pro points. Pro points are part of your program and count toward graduation, so this is not purely goodwill — it is one of the few ways club involvement feeds directly back into your degree. Requirements are not identical across programs, so check with your advisor rather than assuming.

The regular one is Tech Con, an outreach day Mid-State runs each year: high school students come in for a day of hands-on activities to find out what IT actually is. Volunteers help with setup and then run stations — lock picking, soldering, hacking, coding challenges, retro gaming, stickers, social media, and whatever else is running that year.

You do not need to be an expert at a station to run one. You need to be a step ahead of a high schooler who has never seen it before, and several of these are things the club can practise on an activity night first.

It is worth saying what it is like from the other side: for a lot of those high school students, the person running the lock-picking table is the first person they have met who does IT. That is most of why the event works.

Calls for volunteers go out at club meetings, which is a practical reason to be on the roster and paying attention even in weeks when nothing else is happening. Volunteering is always optional and always your choice.

Definition supplied by the advisor 2026-09-06 as the club's long-standing practice — it is not in the constitution's text, so it is not binding until amended. Two things the club still has to settle before this can be proposed: (1) the attendance number, left blank above deliberately rather than invented; (2) how it squares with Article VII §3, which already requires two prior meetings before a large event — if the two set different attendance bars, members will meet one and fail the other. HOW THE ACADEMIC CONDITION IS VERIFIED — settled 2026-09-15, and the reasoning belongs here rather than in the constitution. It runs on self-disclosure: a member failing a course is expected to raise it with the advisor, who may confirm where able and may use the Article VII §2 discretion. The reason it cannot be written as a check the club performs is concrete: the advisor has no access to grades, GPAs or enrolments for courses they do not teach — that is protected student data — and club officers are fellow students who must never be looking at anyone's academic record. A requirement nobody can verify does not go unenforced evenly; it falls on whichever member was honest enough to say something, which is the opposite of what this club wants to do for a student having a hard semester. One consequence to keep in view: good standing is recorded as a label in Engage, so the moment it encodes an academic condition, that label carries academic information to whoever administers the roster. Keep the roster label to the fact of standing, never a reason. If the club ever wants the label to be clean of academic meaning entirely, the way to get there is to drop the academic condition and rely on Article III §2 and Article VII §2, which already exist. The draft keeps the advisor discretion from Article VII §2 on purpose, says plainly that losing good standing does not lose you your membership, and states that standing resets every semester. All three are deliberate: a student having a rough semester is often the one who needs the club most, and a rule written without that flexibility would push away exactly the member it should be holding on to. The standards matter, but they exist to serve students, not to sort them. The semester reset is also what keeps the record small — nothing is ever assessed across more than the current semester, so no long attendance history has to exist anywhere.
